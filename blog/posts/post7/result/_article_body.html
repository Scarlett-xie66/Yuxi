Economic downturns do not leave every worker equally exposed. In a comparison of 251 downturn country-years, men’s employment rates fell more than women’s on average. But a smaller decline for women did not mean that women’s employment improved.

When an economy shrinks, do women’s or men’s employment rates fall more?

Consider two hypothetical downturns of the same size: one centred on factory closures, the other on travel and hospitality. The workers affected could differ even when the fall in national output looks similar. For governments planning job placement or income support, the size of the contraction alone is therefore an incomplete guide to who may need help.

The data offer a way to examine the broader pattern. I match women’s and men’s employment-to-population rates with real GDP growth, producing 1,731 annual country-year changes from 85 countries and territories between 2001 and 2024.1 The analysis compares changes in the two rates within the same country and year; it does not identify which industries caused those changes.

GDP declined in 251 of those records, from 79 of the 85 countries and territories. These are country-years, not 251 different countries: some places experienced several downturn years. Women and men are paired within each country-year, so the comparison tracks whose employment rate changed more under the same national GDP movement.

Men’s employment rates fell further

In the 251 downturn country-years, women’s employment rate fell by an average 0.52 percentage points, compared with 1.24 points for men. Women’s rate changed more favourably than men’s in 187 cases (75%): it either fell less or rose more. Yet women’s rate declined in 166 of the 251 downturn records overall. Each country-year counts equally, so these figures describe the sample average, not a global total of jobs lost.

Spain illustrates why the aggregate comparison is only a starting point. In 2009, the employment-rate declines were 1.76 points for women and 5.93 for men. In 2020, the declines were 1.64 and 2.05 points, respectively. Men’s decline was larger in both years, but the difference was much narrower in 2020. The same country can experience downturns with very different distributions of employment-rate changes.

Deeper contractions bring a wider difference

The raw averages mix mild and severe downturns across countries and years. They tell us what happened in the selected downturn records, but not whether deeper contractions are associated with a wider difference between women and men. A model uses all 1,731 country-year changes—including years when GDP grew—to compare deeper with milder contractions. It accounts for persistent country differences and shifts shared across years, while estimating separate relationships for contractions and expansions.2

When annual GDP growth is 1 percentage point lower—for example, −4% instead of −3%—the model associates that deeper contraction with a further 0.17-point fall in women’s employment rate and a 0.34-point fall in men’s. The estimated male decline is about twice as large. The direct male-minus-female difference is 0.17 points (95% interval: 0.06 to 0.29), which excludes zero. Both rates fall; the relative difference is not a gain in women’s employment.

Figure 2 puts the result in context: both groups’ estimated changes are negative, but the male estimate is further below zero. Unlike the downturn average, this comparison accounts for differences in contraction depth and for country-specific and common year patterns. It therefore adds a measure of how the female–male difference varies with the severity of a downturn. Before 2020, the estimated male-minus-female difference was larger: 0.29 points [0.18, 0.41], so the result is not driven by pandemic downturns alone.

A data-source check on the same 1,718 country-years gives a similar direct difference using ILO-modeled employment series: 0.18 points [0.07, 0.29]. This is a consistency check, not independent proof; modeled series may incorporate macroeconomic information.

Takeaway

In the downturns studied, men’s employment rates fell further than women’s: 1.24 percentage points on average, compared with 0.52 points for women. The difference remained after accounting for country and year patterns. For each additional percentage point of GDP contraction, the estimated decline was 0.34 points for men and 0.17 points for women. The result points to an uneven distribution of employment-rate declines across the two groups. Yet women’s employment rates also fell in many downturns: a smaller decline is not the same as a gain.

Footnotes

  1. Employment-to-population rate: employed people aged 15+ divided by the same-sex population aged 15+. It is not a group’s share of the labor force. Contraction: negative annual real GDP growth. Sources: World Bank and ILO.
  2. Method: coefficients are percentage-point changes per 1-point change in GDP growth. Country and year fixed effects account for persistent country differences and common annual movements. Intervals are clustered by country. Estimates are associations, not causal effects.

Reproducibility

The project keeps the article, code, frozen data and results together:

index.qmd                 # executable entry point
README.md                 # sample, sources and setup
code/                     # fetch, analysis, figures and validation
data/raw/                 # unchanged official API downloads
data/processed/           # matched analysis panel
result/                   # estimates, coverage audit and figures

Keep the complete project folder together. Install Quarto, R with knitr, and the Python packages in requirements.txt; then open index.qmd in RStudio and click Render. The QMD runs the analysis automatically from the saved raw data and regenerates the matched panel, estimates, figures, interactive charts and article. For Anaconda, set BLOG7_PYTHON to the Python executable as explained in the README. To refresh official data first, run code/fetch_data.py. Sources: World Bank real GDP growth and ILO/World Bank national employment series for women and men; the manifest records the downloaded snapshot.
