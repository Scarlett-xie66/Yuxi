How Certain Is the Evidence? A Bootstrap Analysis of Cash Transfers and Women-Owned Businesses in Kenya

Using a 2020 Kenyan experiment to understand confidence intervals and hypothesis tests

SIMULATIONBOOTSTRAPDEVELOPMENT ECONOMICS
AUTHORScarlett Xie
DATAKenya, 2020

In June 2020, women who received a larger cash transfer in Dandora, a neighborhood in Nairobi, Kenya, were more likely to report that their businesses were open. Among those interviewed, 89.9% of the 5,000-shilling group were open, compared with 84.3% of the 500-shilling group—a difference of 5.6 percentage points.

How certain is the observed difference in business-open shares between women who received larger and smaller cash transfers?

A single comparison cannot show how precisely that gap is measured. Resampling provides an interval for the difference, and a simulated no-effect benchmark shows how unusual a gap this large would be under a specified model.1

Two transfers, one economic shock

In May 2020, researchers randomly assigned women microenterprise owners to receive a one-time payment. The larger-transfer group received 5,000 Kenyan shillings (about $50); the comparison group also received money: 500 shillings (about $5), to cover participation and phone costs. Neither payment depended on the owner’s business decisions. To make a common comparison, I use each participant’s first valid interview in June 2020. Among respondents, 258 of 287 businesses in the higher-transfer group were open, compared with 274 of 325 in the comparison group. That is 89.9% versus 84.3%: an observed difference of 5.6 percentage points.

The 5.6-percentage-point difference means that, for every 100 women interviewed, roughly six more businesses were open in the 5,000-shilling group. This comparison is our starting point. Bootstrap resampling helps show how precisely the gap is measured.2

Resampling puts a range around the gap

To gauge how much the 5.6-point gap might vary, I use a method called bootstrap resampling. First, I draw businesses at random from each group, allowing the same business to be selected more than once. Then I calculate the gap again. Repeating these steps 20,000 times produces the range of gaps in Figure 2.

The middle 95% of these gaps runs from 0.4 to 10.8 percentage points. This interval describes the uncertainty around the difference in open-business shares for the June respondent comparison.3

The 95% interval lies above zero, pointing to a higher open-business share in the 5,000-shilling group under this resampling approach. Its lower endpoint is close to zero, so the results are also consistent with a very small difference. A positive interval is evidence of a difference, not proof that a difference or a causal effect must exist.

What a 95% interval captures

The true difference in the population of interest is unknown, so the real sample cannot tell us whether its interval contains that value. Instead, imagine a separate population in which 90% of higher-transfer businesses and 84% of comparison businesses are open: the true difference is six points. I repeatedly draw two independent samples of the same sizes as above and rebuild the bootstrap interval for each pair.4

A 95% procedure does not promise that every interval catches the truth. It describes how often the procedure succeeds over repeated samples under a specified model.

The vertical line in Figure 3 marks a six-point difference that is known because it was set in the simulation. The three red lines shown miss that value; most of the 100 displayed intervals contain it. Of 2,000 intervals, 1,898 capture that value and 102 miss it. The 94.9% coverage is close to the intended 95% for these simulated conditions. It is a check of the interval procedure, not evidence that the real Kenyan difference was six points.

What if the transfer made no difference?

The real June data show a 5.6-point difference between the two groups’ open-business shares. How unusual would a gap this large be if the extra money changed no one’s reported business status? To explore that question, I start with a hypothetical world in which the larger transfer has no effect on any June respondent.5

Keeping the observed business statuses and group sizes fixed, I repeatedly shuffle group labels and calculate the difference in open-business shares. Even under this no-effect assumption, the simulated groups need not have exactly the same share: chance can place more open businesses in one group than the other.

Most simulated gaps are near zero. That does not prove the real effect is zero: the figure shows what might happen if the larger payment made no difference. Even then, random grouping can produce a positive or negative gap.

Before comparing the observed result, we set a 5% threshold and mark the sufficiently extreme gaps in red. The observed +5.6-point gap falls in the red rejection region. The p-value asks a related question: how often do simulated gaps fall at least as far from zero as the observed gap? Counting both directions gives approximately 4.6%, or about 46 in 1,000 shuffles. This is relatively uncommon under the no-effect model, not a 4.6% probability that the model is true.

Here the simulated difference can take only certain values, so the rejection region cannot contain exactly 5% of the null distribution. Its exact probability is about 4.2%; the 4.6% p-value is estimated from 20,000 simulations and varies with the random draws.

The result is an illustration of hypothesis testing for June respondents. Because response occurred after payment and the original assignment has not been fully reconstructed, it is not an exact test for everyone in the experiment.

Takeaway

In June 2020, 89.9% of responding businesses in the higher-transfer group were open, versus 84.3% in the comparison group. The observed gap was 5.6 percentage points; its bootstrap interval was 0.4 to 10.8 points. Under the simplified no-effect simulation, a gap at least as extreme appeared in 4.6% of reallocations. The data point to a higher open-business share among June respondents who received 5,000 shillings. The interval and no-effect simulation help assess that difference, while the size of any causal effect remains uncertain.

Reproducibility

The analysis uses the public replication data accompanying Brooks and colleagues’ study. This HTML is a preview. A complete reproducible project containing the code, data and generated results has not yet been supplied.

The project is intended to follow this structure:

index.qmd                 # article and render entry point
index.html                # rendered article
README.md                 # dependencies and replication instructions
code/                     # data cleaning, simulation and figures
data/
├── raw/                  # original study data and source information
└── processed/            # analysis sample
result/
├── figures/              # generated figures
└── tables/               # sample counts and simulation results

Once the project is completed, rendering index.qmd should rebuild the analysis, figures and article using project-relative paths. The source is Donovan (2022), Mendeley Data, version 1, CC BY 4.0. The preview uses a fixed random seed of 4082026.
