When a young child needs care, someone has to provide it or pay for it. That constraint matters for household budgets, employers and the design of child care policy. Yet a simple claim about “parents’ employment” can hide two quite different patterns. This post asks: how does employment differ between adults with a young child at home and adults without a minor child at home, and is the comparison the same for women and men?

The 2025 US Current Population Survey offers a useful snapshot. It cannot follow the same parents before and after a birth, but it can show where the labor market differences lie. I start with employment, then separate labor force participation from unemployment, and finally check whether broad age and education differences account for the contrast.

The employment split

# Step 5: recode child age, employment and controls; retain eligible adults.
# EMPSTAT general code 1 = employed; 2 = unemployed; 3 = not in labor force.
# YNGCH 99 = no identified own child living in the household.
# EDUC 999 = missing or unknown. EDUC 000 may also mean no schooling, so retain it.
cps <- cps_raw |>
  dplyr::mutate(
    sex = factor(as.numeric(SEX), levels = c(1, 2), labels = c("Men", "Women")),
    child_group = dplyr::case_when(
      YNGCH == 99 | YNGCH >= 18 ~ "no_minor",
      YNGCH >= 0 & YNGCH <= 5 ~ "age0_5",
      YNGCH >= 6 & YNGCH <= 12 ~ "age6_12",
      YNGCH >= 13 & YNGCH <= 17 ~ "age13_17",
      TRUE ~ NA_character_
    ),
    child_group = factor(child_group,
                         levels = c("no_minor", "age0_5", "age6_12", "age13_17")),
    status = floor(as.numeric(EMPSTAT) / 10),
    employed = as.numeric(status == 1),
    labor_force = as.numeric(status %in% c(1, 2)),
    unemployed = as.numeric(status == 2),
    age_band = factor(dplyr::case_when(
      AGE <= 34 ~ "25-34", AGE <= 44 ~ "35-44", TRUE ~ "45-54"
    ), levels = c("25-34", "35-44", "45-54")),
    college = factor(ifelse(EDUC >= 111, "BA or higher", "Below BA"),
                     levels = c("Below BA", "BA or higher"))
  ) |>
  dplyr::filter(AGE >= 25, AGE <= 54, !is.na(sex), !is.na(child_group),
                status %in% c(1, 2, 3), !is.na(EDUC), EDUC != 999,
                !is.na(ASECWT), ASECWT > 0)
if (!nrow(cps)) stop("No eligible records: inspect IPUMS variable codes.")

# Step 7: estimate eight group rates and their design-based confidence intervals.
rates <- design |>
  dplyr::group_by(sex, child_group) |>
  dplyr::summarise(
    employment = srvyr::survey_mean(employed, vartype = "ci", na.rm = TRUE),
    participation = srvyr::survey_mean(labor_force, vartype = "ci", na.rm = TRUE),
    unemployment = srvyr::survey_ratio(unemployed, labor_force,
                                        vartype = "ci", na.rm = TRUE),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) |>
  as.data.frame()
readr::write_csv(rates, file.path(derived_dir, "weighted_rates.csv"))
pick_rate <- function(s, g, col = "employment") {
  row <- rates[rates$sex == s & rates$child_group == g, , drop = FALSE]
  if (nrow(row) != 1L) stop("An expected group is missing: ", s, ", ", g)
  as.numeric(row[[col]])
}

Among men, the corresponding rates are 93.6% and 80.9%. The comparison runs in opposite directions: -9.9 percentage points for women and +12.7 percentage points for men. Each bar is a CPS person-weighted population estimate, rather than a count of surveyed people.

The employment rate can fall because fewer adults look for paid work, because more job seekers cannot find work, or both. Figure 2 places everyone in one of three mutually exclusive states. Among women with a child aged 0 to 5 at home, 69.8% are in the labor force, versus 80.3% in the reference group. Their unemployment rates, measured among labor force participants, are 3.0% and 3.5%. The conspicuous employment difference therefore sits mainly alongside a participation difference, not a higher unemployment rate.

Does the divide remain after adjustment?

The groups differ in more than family circumstances. For a descriptive check, I estimate a survey-weighted linear probability model. The outcome is one if a person is employed and zero otherwise. Let \(F_i\) indicate a woman, \(C_{ig}\) indicate the youngest-child group \(g\) (relative to no minor child at home), \(A_i\) denote age-band indicators and \(B_i\) indicate a bachelor’s degree or higher. The estimated equation is

\[ \Pr(E_i=1 \mid F_i,C_i,A_i,B_i) = \alpha + \beta F_i + \sum_g \gamma_g C_{ig} + \sum_g \delta_g(F_i C_{ig}) + \boldsymbol{\theta}' A_i + \lambda B_i. \]

For the 0-to-5 group, \(\gamma_{0-5}\) is the adjusted difference for men; \(\gamma_{0-5}+\delta_{0-5}\) is the difference for women; and \(\delta_{0-5}\) is the difference between those two differences. The reference person is a man aged 25 to 34, without a bachelor’s degree and without an own minor child at home. Model coefficients are percentage-point changes when multiplied by 100, not percentage changes.

Show R code 
model <- survey::svyglm(
  employed ~ sex * child_group + age_band + college,
  design = design, family = stats::gaussian()
)
b <- stats::coef(model)
V <- stats::vcov(model)
ci_critical <- stats::qt(.975, df = 159) # 160 person replicates, 159 design df.
men_term <- "child_groupage0_5"
interaction_term <- "sexWomen:child_groupage0_5"
stopifnot(all(c(men_term, interaction_term) %in% names(b)))
contrast <- function(label, terms) {
  cvec <- stats::setNames(rep(0, length(b)), names(b))
  cvec[terms] <- 1
  estimate <- sum(cvec * b)
  se <- sqrt(as.numeric(t(cvec) %*% V %*% cvec))
  data.frame(group = label, estimate_pp = 100 * estimate,
             lower_pp = 100 * (estimate - ci_critical * se),
             upper_pp = 100 * (estimate + ci_critical * se), se_pp = 100 * se)
}
adjusted <- dplyr::bind_rows(
  contrast("Men", men_term),
  contrast("Women", c(men_term, interaction_term)),
  contrast("Women minus men", interaction_term)
)
readr::write_csv(adjusted, file.path(derived_dir, "adjusted_gaps.csv"))
capture.output(summary(model), file = file.path(derived_dir, "model_summary.txt"))
capture.output(sessionInfo(), file = file.path(derived_dir, "session_info.txt"))
adj_w <- adjusted$estimate_pp[adjusted$group == "Women"]
adj_m <- adjusted$estimate_pp[adjusted$group == "Men"]

The adjusted employment divide

Figure 3 plots the first two rows of the table. Each dot is an estimated difference from adults of the same recorded sex with no minor child at home; the horizontal line is its 95% confidence interval. Zero means no adjusted difference. The third table row compares the two dots statistically and is the young-child interaction coefficient. Showing it in the table avoids mistaking that interaction for a third employment rate.

The adjusted difference is -10.7 percentage points for women and +11.2 percentage points for men. The difference between them is -21.9 percentage points (95% interval: -23.9 to -20.0 percentage points). The opposite signs persist after these controls. That is a sharper description of the employment divide, not an estimate of what a birth would do to a particular person’s job prospects.

Takeaway

A divide the data can reveal, but not explain

The survey reveals two distinct labor market patterns around young children: women in the 0-to-5 household group are less likely to be employed than women without a minor child at home, while men in the corresponding group are more likely to be employed. For women, participation rather than unemployment is the more informative margin. The difference survives broad age and education adjustment. It points to an important question for child care and work policy: what combination of care costs, household specialization, earnings and selection into parenthood lies behind it? This cross-sectional comparison cannot distinguish those mechanisms or support a causal claim.

Behind the numbers: Data and replication

Data note. The API downloads the full 2025 CPS Annual Social and Economic Supplement person extract, including children and older adults. It contains 142,125 unweighted person records. After restricting to ages 25-54, 55,003 remain; after removing invalid or missing analysis fields and nonpositive person weights, 54,607 remain. The latter is a subset of the former, not a count of the US population. Population rates use the ASEC person weight.

The youngest-child measure identifies a person’s youngest own child at home, including biological, step and adopted children as defined by IPUMS. “No minor child at home” may include people with adult children or children living elsewhere; it is not synonymous with never having children. The CPS sex field records male or female; it does not measure gender identity. These details matter when interpreting the comparison.

Source field Purpose
AGE, SEX, YNGCH Age restriction, recorded sex and youngest own child at home
EMPSTAT, EDUC Employment status and education control
ASECWT ASEC person weight for population rates and the regression
REPWTP1 through REPWTP160 Person replicate weights for survey uncertainty

The QMD requests sample cps2025_03s using the IPUMS API, caches the licensed person data privately outside the website, recodes the variables, computes weighted rates, fits the interaction model and saves the following outputs. The source is IPUMS CPS, based on the Census Bureau and Bureau of Labor Statistics survey; see the youngest-child definition, person weights and replicate-weight guidance.
